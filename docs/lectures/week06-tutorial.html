Week 6 Tutorial: Design in Practice

Wednesday tutorial · reinforces Monday’s lecture on reading designs in the published literature

Goals

  • Practice identifying the elements of an experimental design from a methods section, rather than from a textbook diagram
  • Build a habit of asking the same short list of questions of any paper you read

A framework for reading a methods section

Whenever you read the methods of a paper, try to answer these questions, in this order:

  1. What is the experimental unit? The thing that was independently assigned to a treatment — not necessarily the thing that was measured (recall Week 5’s pseudoreplication discussion).
  2. What are the treatments/groups, and how many levels does each factor have?
  3. How many experimental units per treatment (the replication)?
  4. Was assignment to treatment randomized? If so, how — and if not, is it an observational study instead?
  5. Was there any blocking, matching, or other control for a known source of variation?
  6. What was the response variable, and how was it measured?
  7. What statistical test or model did the authors use to analyze it — and does that choice match the design (matching what we covered in Weeks 5 and 7 onward)?

Worked example (hypothetical)

Consider a hypothetical study: researchers want to know whether a soil amendment increases seedling survival in a restoration planting. They have 40 plots across a hillside, and randomly assign half to receive the amendment and half to serve as controls. Survival is recorded after one growing season.

Applying the framework:

  1. Experimental unit: the plot (not the individual seedling — seedlings within a plot share soil and microclimate, so they aren’t independent).
  2. Treatments: one factor, two levels (amendment vs. control).
  3. Replication: 20 plots per treatment.
  4. Randomization: yes — amendment assigned randomly across plots.
  5. Blocking: none mentioned; if the hillside has a known elevation gradient, that’s a missed opportunity to block.
  6. Response variable: proportion of seedlings surviving per plot.
  7. Analysis: a logistic regression or a t-test on arcsine-transformed proportions would both be defensible choices here — this connects to Week 11’s lecture on the logistic alternative to lm().

Exercise: find your own paper

NoteExercise 1

Find one primary research article in your own subfield that includes an experiment (not a purely observational study). Using the framework above, answer all seven questions for that paper’s design. If any answer isn’t clear from the methods section, say so — that’s itself a useful finding about how clearly (or not) the study reported its design.

NoteExercise 2

Would you have designed the study differently? Propose one concrete change (e.g., adding a blocking factor, increasing replication, changing what’s randomized) and explain what it would buy you.

Wrap-up

This tutorial is deliberately close to this week’s assignment — the assignment asks you to do a more polished, formally written version of exactly this exercise with a paper of your choosing.
